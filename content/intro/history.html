History of Hacking

The Evolution of Hacking

Hacking did not begin with computers. The hacker ethos — curiosity, exploration, and pushing systems beyond their intended limits — traces back to early telephone networks. Understanding this history provides context for modern security practices and helps you appreciate how attack techniques evolved.

Timeline of Key Events

1970s — Phone Phreaking

Before computer hacking, there was "phreaking" — exploiting telephone systems. John Draper ("Captain Crunch") discovered that a toy whistle from a cereal box produced a 2600 Hz tone that could manipulate AT&T's switching systems, allowing free long-distance calls. Steve Wozniak and Steve Jobs famously built and sold "blue boxes" that replicated this technique before founding Apple.

1988 — The Morris Worm

Robert Tappan Morris, a Cornell graduate student, released the first major Internet worm. It exploited vulnerabilities in Unix sendmail, fingerd, and weak passwords via rsh/rexec. The worm infected approximately 6,000 machines — about 10% of the Internet at the time — and caused an estimated $10-100 million in damages. Morris was the first person convicted under the CFAA. The incident led to the creation of CERT/CC (Computer Emergency Response Team).

1990s — Kevin Mitnick

Kevin Mitnick became the most famous hacker of the 1990s, breaking into networks of major corporations including Nokia, Motorola, and Sun Microsystems. He used social engineering as much as technical exploits. After a highly publicized pursuit by the FBI (with assistance from security researcher Tsutomu Shimomura), Mitnick was arrested in 1995 and served five years in prison. He later became a security consultant and author, demonstrating that the line between attacker and defender is often about choices, not skill.

2003 — SQL Slammer Worm

The SQL Slammer worm exploited a buffer overflow in Microsoft SQL Server 2000. In just 10 minutes, it infected 75,000 servers and caused widespread Internet slowdowns. The entire worm payload was only 376 bytes — small enough to fit in a single UDP packet. It demonstrated how a simple vulnerability could cascade into a global incident.

2010 — Stuxnet

Stuxnet was a sophisticated worm widely attributed to the United States and Israel, designed to sabotage Iran's nuclear enrichment program. It targeted Siemens SCADA systems controlling centrifuges at the Natanz facility, causing them to spin at destructive speeds while reporting normal readings to operators. Stuxnet used four zero-day exploits and stolen digital certificates. It was the first known cyberweapon used to cause physical damage and marked the beginning of nation-state cyber warfare.

2011 — Anonymous & LulzSec

The hacktivist collective Anonymous and its offshoot LulzSec conducted high-profile attacks against governments, corporations, and organizations. LulzSec compromised Sony Pictures (leaking 1 million accounts), PBS, the CIA website, and more — often using basic techniques like SQL injection. Their motto "doing it for the lulz" highlighted how even unsophisticated attacks could cause massive damage when basic security hygiene was ignored.

2010s-Present — Bug Bounty Era

The launch of platforms like HackerOne (2012) and Bugcrowd (2012) transformed vulnerability research into a legitimate career. Companies began paying researchers for finding bugs instead of prosecuting them. Google's Vulnerability Reward Program has paid out over $40 million. The U.S. Department of Defense launched "Hack the Pentagon" in 2016. Today, thousands of organizations run bug bounty programs, and top researchers earn six-figure incomes from bounties alone.

Lesson from history: The most devastating attacks often exploit well-known, preventable vulnerabilities. SQL injection — discovered in the late 1990s — is still in the OWASP Top 10 today. Understanding the past helps you recognize patterns that repeat in modern systems.
