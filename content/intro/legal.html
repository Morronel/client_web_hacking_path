Legal & Ethics in Security Testing

Why Law & Ethics Matter

Before you touch a single packet or write a single exploit, you must understand the legal boundaries of security testing. The line between a penetration tester and a criminal is authorization. The same technical action — scanning a network, exploiting a vulnerability, extracting data — can be either a paid professional engagement or a felony depending on whether you have permission.

Key Cybercrime Laws

Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — United States

Enacted in 1986 and amended multiple times, the CFAA criminalizes "intentionally accessing a computer without authorization or exceeding authorized access." Penalties range from fines to 20+ years in prison for repeat offenses. The CFAA has been criticized for its broad language — even violating a website's Terms of Service has been argued as a CFAA violation.

Computer Misuse Act (CMA) — United Kingdom

The CMA (1990) creates three primary offenses: unauthorized access to computer material (up to 2 years), unauthorized access with intent to commit further offenses (up to 5 years), and unauthorized modification of computer material (up to 10 years). The act applies regardless of whether the attacker is in the UK, as long as the target system is.

Budapest Convention on Cybercrime

The first international treaty addressing cybercrime, signed in 2001. It harmonizes national laws, improves investigative techniques, and establishes international cooperation mechanisms. Over 65 countries have ratified the convention. It covers illegal access, data interference, system interference, misuse of devices, and computer-related fraud.

Jurisdiction matters: Cybercrime laws vary by country. Testing a server in another country may subject you to that country's laws. Always know where your target infrastructure is located and which laws apply.

Authorization Types

Written Scope of Work

Professional penetration tests require a signed contract (sometimes called a "Statement of Work" or "Rules of Engagement") that explicitly defines what you are allowed to test, which methods are permitted, the time window for testing, and what is out of scope. This document is your legal shield.

Bug Bounty Programs

Companies like Google, Microsoft, and thousands of others run bug bounty programs through platforms such as HackerOne and Bugcrowd. These programs provide a legal framework for security researchers to find and report vulnerabilities in exchange for monetary rewards. Each program has its own scope, rules, and safe harbor provisions.

Key elements of a bug bounty policy: in-scope domains/applications, out-of-scope areas, prohibited actions (e.g., social engineering employees, DoS), disclosure timelines, and reward tiers.

Rules of Engagement (RoE)

Rules of engagement define the boundaries of a security assessment: testing hours, IP ranges, whether social engineering is allowed, escalation procedures if critical vulnerabilities are found, and emergency contacts. Violating the RoE can void your authorization and expose you to legal liability.

Ethical Hacking Principles

Ethical hackers follow a set of core principles that distinguish them from malicious actors:

  • Get explicit permission — never test without written authorization
  • Stay within scope — only test what you are authorized to test
  • Do no harm — avoid actions that could cause damage, data loss, or service disruption
  • Protect data — if you access sensitive data during testing, treat it as confidential
  • Report everything — document and disclose all findings to the client
  • Respect privacy — do not access personal data beyond what is necessary to demonstrate the vulnerability

Disclosure Models

Model Description Pros Cons
Responsible Disclosure Report to vendor first, give them time to fix (typically 90 days), then publish Gives vendor time to patch; users protected Vendor may ignore or delay; researcher has no leverage
Full Disclosure Publish vulnerability details immediately to the public Pressures vendor to act quickly; community can defend Attackers get exploit details before patch exists
Coordinated Disclosure Work with vendor and possibly a coordinator (e.g., CERT) to time the disclosure with a patch release Best balance of transparency and safety Requires cooperation from all parties
Industry standard: Most bug bounty platforms follow coordinated disclosure with a 90-day window. Google's Project Zero popularized this timeline — after 90 days, the vulnerability is published regardless of patch status.
